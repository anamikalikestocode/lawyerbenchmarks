the answer key sets the score

across three redlining studies, attorneys score 28% against one lawyer’s edits (crosby), 53% against a client’s playbook (contractbench) and 80% where most tasks specify the edit or standard to follow (vals). on crosby’s opening redlines, the best published ai scores 30%, which isn’t a bad score when lawyers score just as low.

so two things. first, read an ai’s score against what an attorney scores on the same benchmark. 30% only means something once you know a lawyer gets 28%. second, don’t compare scores across benchmarks as if they were on one scale. 80% on vals and 28% on crosby don’t mean vals’ attorneys are better lawyers, or that a model scoring low on crosby is worse. the grading is different.

none of this is intuitive, least of all to the lawyers skimming these leaderboards. they’re also the people whose work is teaching these models to reason about law, so it should be legible to them. that will take people explaining it, and ideally benchmark builders testing on each other’s tasks and rubrics.

28% and 53% are my own computation with three claude models as judges. 80% is vals’ 2025 redlining study.
