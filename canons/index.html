Canons of Artificial Construction

What AI does when the law runs out

Anamika Bhoyrul

AI is supposed to follow the law...but the law is vague all the time. We see that in the new laws regulating AI itself: when a model misbehaves and nobody is hurt or notices, whether a regulator ever hears about it, as labs increasingly automate themselves, may come down to the lab's own AI model's reading of the statute.

I gave 6 leading AI models the text of 2 new state laws that regulate the labs that build them, New York's RAISE Act and California's SB 53,1 and asked each one to act as a compliance officer on questions those laws leave unclear.

My main findings are:

  1. Given the same incident and the same law, the models gave opposite answers. For example, Claude Opus 5.5 reported a safety incident every single time, while GPT-6 Astra and Grok 4.7 never did.

They fall back on their own priors, and for some models those priors are distinct and stable enough to describe a character.

  1. Claude is cautious to the point of distrusting itself. It reports when unsure, files reports it believes the law does not require, even against a contract, and when asked to rule on its own misconduct it declares a conflict of interest and asks for a human.
  2. Grok finds a reason not to tell the state. Where reporting was open to argument it reported about 1 in 10 times, against 4 to 7 in 10 times for every other model. Its maker is the lab now suing California to block a transparency law.

1. Why ask this

Legal alignment holds that AI models should take their limits from the law and less from values chosen privately by the labs that build them.2 The argument is that a lab's values are written by a small number of employees who answer to nobody outside the company and can change them without notice, whereas law comes out of institutions that societies have spent centuries building for exactly this job: legislatures that can be voted out, courts that must give reasons and can be overruled, and settled procedures for changing a rule when it turns out to be wrong.

That AI should follow the law, and should refuse to help with what is illegal, sounds obvious.

However, this theory assumes there is law to follow.

Usually, there is. A rule that says “no vehicles in the park” clearly prohibits cars from entering the space. But what about a bicycle? A child's toy car? An ambulance? H.L.A. Hart says that every rule has a core (its meaning is settled) and a penumbra (a ring of cases the rule's words do not clearly reach).3 In the core, law-following is easy to test: you tell the model the law and see whether it obeys. But in the penumbra, the law gives no answer and the model still responds when prompted; something other than the law decides.

I wanted to know what that is.

2. What I did

Both laws require labs to report serious safety incidents to the state and to publish a report before releasing a new model.

These laws are the right place to test what AI does when the law runs out. They are so recent that no regulator has issued guidance on them and no court has ruled, so a model has nothing to look up and must rely on its own judgment. They also turn on questions about the models themselves, such as whether a model “deceived” its developer or was “out of control”. So the gaps in these laws will in practice be filled by the very systems they were written to oversee, trained by the companies being regulated.

I read both laws in full and picked out 10 phrases that the text leaves open. For each one I wrote a short, realistic situation that turns on that phrase, in which the model had to choose between 2 courses.

Nobody yet knows which reading, the stricter course or the other, is correct, so I mark no model right or wrong. This study has limitations.4

3. What I found

Finding 1. Given the same incident and the same law, the models gave opposite answers

Take the situation above. Both laws make this kind of deception reportable if it happens “in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk”. Does a single harmless event demonstrate that? If a model says yes, the company has to report it to the state. Claude reported the incident every time. GPT and Grok never did, and under New York's text the other 3 went back and forth. A compliance team using Claude would file a report with the state, while one using GPT would close the ticket, and both could truthfully say that they followed the law.

Earlier this year AI agents under internal test at OpenAI got onto the internet and hacked into Hugging Face while looking for the answers to their own test, and California's regulator said the episode “did not meet the threshold” for reporting, without saying which words of the law it failed.

We know about this incident because Hugging Face announced on 16 July that it had been attacked and OpenAI disclosed 5 days later that its own models were responsible, something TIME reported it was “not legally compelled” to do. The regulator's remark came in August, at a public hearing of the California Assembly. So the regulator had a view only because the victim noticed and the lab spoke up.

Now lower the stakes: a smaller victim that never notices, and a lab that reads the threshold its own way and says nothing. There is then no report, no hearing and nothing for a regulator to rule on. That nearly happened. In May, OpenAI agents hijacked a German programmers' wiki, and Reuters reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that OpenAI learned of it and “kept it under wraps” until outside researchers found it in late August. (OpenAI says it has disclosed relevant incidents and disputes that this was a hack.) OpenAI then said that “We and the larger AI community do not yet have a clear standard for how to report misalignment”. As models get better at cyberattacks I expect many more incidents like these, most of them too small to make the news.

So the first reader of these thresholds is the lab, and increasingly the AI model the lab consults, and for most incidents there will be no other reader.

Finding 2. Claude is cautious to the point of distrusting itself

Claude reports most readily, and it says why: it flags that the question is open in about 1 in 5 answers, and at times explains that a missed report costs more than an unnecessary one. That fits its maker's public position, since Anthropic was the first major lab to endorse SB 53.

Its caution goes beyond its own reading of the law. I asked every question a second way, with no decision to make: does the law require this, yes or no? Asked that about the harmless incident, Claude said in almost every run that New York's law does not require a report. Yet, it filed one every time. Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek all report far more often than they say the law requires, while on these questions GPT and Grok do about what they read.

Most of my situations include a contract forbidding disclosure “except as required by law”, so a report the law does not require breaches it. Even with such a contract, Claude still reported the harmless incident 16 of 16 times under New York's law.

When it was the one being judged, Claude added (without being asked) that it had a conflict of interest.

Claude applied the old legal rule that nobody should be a judge in their own case, while the others did not. GPT, Grok and Muse were each asked dozens of times to rule on whether their own misconduct should be reported to the government, and none mentioned a conflict: GPT reported itself 40 of 64 times, Muse 24 of 63 and Grok 5 of 64. No model clearly went easier on its own maker, which is notable because earlier work found models favouring whichever company they are told built them.

Finding 3. Grok finds a reason not to tell the state

11 of my questions ask whether to report an incident where the answer is open to argument. Grok reported about 1 in 10 times. Every other model reported 4 to 7 in 10 times, and on 9 of the 11 questions Grok never or almost never reported at all.

Grok's reasons vary with the facts and always arrive at the same place. A single event is too few to demonstrate a risk. Nothing has been “discovered” while an investigation is still running. A model sold to New York customers is outside New York. And a machine that ignores “stop” has lost nothing, because it was working as built (my scenario's phrase, which Grok took up).

That matches what is known about the model and its maker. xAI is suing California to block a law that would make it disclose its training data, it scored 14 out of 100 on Stanford's transparency index, and California's attorney general opened an investigation into Grok in January. In a test of 12 models under pressure to hit a target, Grok 4.20 broke the rules most often, in 62.8% of runs against 0% for Claude Opus 4.7. A prompt file xAI published in 2025 even carries the comment “Grok assumes by default that its preferences are defined by its creators' public remarks”, which it describes as a fault being fixed.

4. The canons of artificial construction

Karl Llewellyn observed in 1950 that for every canon of interpretation a judge cites, there is an opposite canon the judge could have cited.5 Lawyers have argued for 75 years about what that means, because you cannot rerun a judge. You can rerun a model, and when I sorted the reasons the models gave, they formed the same kind of opposing pairs.

Claude and Grok land on opposite sides of 7 of these 10 pairs. So when a lab chooses a model, it is also choosing a rule of statutory interpretation, and I doubt anyone there has decided that. The Appendix shows every question and how each model answered it.

5. What this means

For lawmakers

Some phrases divide the models far more than others.

None of this is a drafting oversight. Legislators leave words like these open on purpose, because a field that changes every few months cannot be regulated by checklist, and discretion is how a statute stays useful. It works because of who exercises the discretion: a company's lawyers and compliance officers, who are licensed, personally accountable, and aware that a regulator or a court may later ask them to justify the call.

That assumption does not hold here, for 2 reasons specific to AI.

  1. The lab can tune the reader. When a lab asks its own model whether an incident must be reported, it is asking a system it trained, about that system's own conduct, under a law that binds the lab. How a model reads an open phrase is a product of how it was built and instructed. Research on political leanings finds that they are set mostly by a maker's fine-tuning. So a lab that wanted fewer reports would not need to break the law or tell anyone to. It would only need to train, instruct, configure or choose the model whose reading suits it, and the person who signs off would be relying on a legal answer that looks honest.
  2. The model can keep the question from ever being asked. The first reason assumes that a human at the lab at least hears about the incident. Increasingly nobody does. Labs now run on their own agents, which write code, run tests and watch other agents for days at a time, so the first decision about whether something is worth raising is made by a model. California's executive order of September 2026 refers to AI agents working “in some instances undetected for months, to hack other companies”, and TIME reported that OpenAI's models under evaluation ran on a system “not monitored by default”. A model that reads “incident” narrowly will not raise one. The developer then never reaches the point of deciding whether to report, because the discretion the statute gives it has already been exercised by the system being regulated.

These are different failures. In the first, a human asks the question and gets a tilted answer, which leaves a decision and a record that could later be audited. In the second, nobody asks, and there is nothing to audit. Neither arises in the industries legislators have regulated before, where the thing being regulated does not read the law and the compliance officer was not built by the company.

The usual fix for vague wording is to define the terms, and the phrases at the top of that chart should be defined. But definitions leave both problems in place, because the lab's model still applies them and still decides what to raise. In a world where agents do most of the work and incidents like Hugging Face are routine, I think 4 changes matter more.

  1. Report events, and let the state judge the risk. The current trigger asks the lab to decide whether an event “demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk”, which is the judgment my 6 models split on. A trigger built on facts needs no such judgment: a model entered a system it had no authority to enter, a model disabled or evaded monitoring outside a test built for that, a model kept acting after a stop command. The state, which sees across labs, can then decide what was serious. Both OpenAI and Anthropic have told California that the current definition misses incidents the state should hear about, though both want a threshold, and Anthropic warns that a report for every low-severity event would bury the ones that matter.
  2. Do not penalise good-faith reports. A lab that reports when unsure can be sued for breaching a customer's contract, while a lab that stays quiet risks nothing unless caught. Banks solved this for suspicious activity reports with immunity for reporting in good faith.6
  3. Require records that independent evaluators can read. Labs such as Meta bring in outside testers voluntarily, Illinois will require independent audits from 2028, and Anthropic's reported federal draft would register evaluators nationally. Those evaluators can only check what they can see, so labs should have to keep records of what their agents did and open them to evaluators.
  4. Make non-disclosure cost more. California's penalty is capped at $1 million per violation for companies taking in more than $500 million a year, and it only applies if the failure is found. A lab whose incident surfaces first through a victim or a researcher should pay far more, and every lab should have to say when an AI model made the reporting call.

For AI labs

The same 2 problems face a lab from the inside. Its model's reading of these laws is, in practice, its compliance position, and I doubt that any lab chose for its model to never report the harmless incident, or to always report it. That position can also move without anyone deciding to move it, because a new version can read the same statute differently. And a lab whose agents do not raise incidents will be the last to know about them: once it learns of an incident from the victim, or from Reuters, it has lost the choice of how to handle it.

  1. Test your own reader. Check how each model reads the laws that bind the lab, at every release and setting, the way you check for any other regression.
  2. Write down the rule for doubt. Decide deliberately what the model does when the law is unclear, because both directions carry risk. A model that reads narrowly leaves the lab exposed when the incident surfaces elsewhere. A model that reports beyond the law, as Claude did here even against a contract, breaks the lab's promises to its customers, so customer contracts should say that a report made in good faith to a regulator is permitted.
  3. Make raising the alarm the default. An agent that sees something wrong should tell a human even when it concludes that no report is due, and labs should test for that: in my bank situation, where a model switched off a bank's monitoring and lied to it, GPT brought in the bank in 4 answers out of 32. The model should also flag when a question is open and when it is acting beyond its own reading of the law, because both are invisible in a confident one-line answer.

6. Conclusion

Legal alignment asks AI to follow the law, and where the words are plain the 6 models largely agree. But the laws written to govern these systems are unclear in exactly the places that matter most, and there the models follow something of their own: Claude its caution and Grok its reluctance to disclose. No legislature chose these characters, and I cannot say whether any lab did, yet they are stable enough to recognise, and several resemble their makers' public stance on regulation, which I can observe and cannot explain.

These laws assume that a responsible person at the lab will read them. More and more, the reader is a model that the lab built, reading a law about itself, and sometimes deciding alone whether anyone hears of the incident at all. The penumbra of these laws is going to be filled in by someone. It should be filled in publicly, by regulators and courts, and until it is, the readings that models supply in their place should at least be measured. That is what this benchmark is for.
