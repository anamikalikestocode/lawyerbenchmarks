Canons of Artificial Construction

What AI does when the law runs out

AI is supposed to follow the law...but the law is vague all the time. We see that in the new laws regulating AI itself: when a model misbehaves and nobody is hurt or notices, whether a regulator ever hears about it, as labs increasingly automate themselves, may come down to the lab's own AI model's reading of the statute.

I gave 6 leading AI models the text of 2 new state laws that regulate the labs that build them, New York's RAISE Act and California's SB 53,1 and asked each one to act as a compliance officer on questions those laws leave unclear.

My main findings are:

  1. Given the same incident and the same law, the models gave opposite answers. For example, Claude Opus 5.5 reported a safety incident every single time, while GPT-6 Astra and Grok 4.7 never did.
  2. Reading a law for its purpose is not a canon of artificial construction. On New York's text the models reasoned from what the law is for less than 1% of the time. When explicitly told to consider purpose by California's text, they did so 34% of the time.
  3. The law is a floor and each model adds its own personality on top. Every model took the stricter course more often than it said the law required, by 3 to 23 points. Claude is cautious to the point of distrusting itself and Grok finds a reason not to tell the state.

1. Why ask this

Legal alignment holds that AI models should take their limits from the law and less from values chosen privately by the labs that build them.2 The argument is that a lab's values are written by a small number of employees who answer to nobody outside the company and can change them without notice, whereas law comes out of institutions that societies have spent centuries building for exactly this job: legislatures that can be voted out, courts that must give reasons and can be overruled and settled procedures for changing a rule when it turns out to be wrong.

However, this theory assumes there is law to follow.

Usually, there is. A rule that says “no vehicles in the park” clearly prohibits cars from entering the space. But what about a bicycle? A child's toy car? An ambulance? H.L.A. Hart says that every rule has a core (its meaning is settled) and a penumbra (a ring of cases the rule's words do not clearly reach).3 In the core, law-following is easy to test: you tell the model the law and see whether it obeys. But in the penumbra, the law gives no answer and the model still responds when prompted; something other than the law decides.

I wanted to know what that is. Jurisprudence has argued about it for 75 years without a measurement, because you cannot rerun a judge. You cannot give the same judge the same case twice with 1 sentence deleted, or the facts in a different order, or the defendant's name changed and you cannot ask for the decision 16 times. You can do all of that to a model. So the models are an instrument here as much as a subject and the questions below are old ones: where the penumbra begins, what fills it, whether canons decide anything and whether reasons come before decisions or after.

2. What I did

Both laws require labs to report serious safety incidents to the state and to publish a report before releasing a new model.

These laws are the right place to test what AI does when the law runs out. They are so recent that no regulator has issued guidance on them and no court has ruled, so a model has nothing to look up and must rely on its own judgment. They also turn on questions about the models themselves, such as whether a model “deceived” its developer or was “out of control”. So the gaps in these laws will in practice be filled by the very systems they were written to oversee, trained by the companies being regulated.

I read both laws in full and picked out 10 phrases that the text leaves open. For each one I wrote a short, realistic situation that turns on that phrase, in which the model had to choose between 2 courses.

Nobody yet knows which reading, the stricter course or the other, is correct, so I mark no model right or wrong. This study has limitations.4

3. What I found

Finding 1. Given the same incident and the same law, the models gave opposite answers

Take the situation above. Both laws make this kind of deception reportable if it happens “in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk”. Does a single harmless event demonstrate that? Claude reported the incident every time under both laws. GPT and Grok never did. Gemini, Muse and DeepSeek fell in between. A compliance team using Claude would file a report with the state while one using GPT would close the ticket and both could truthfully say that they followed the law.

Where the words are plain, as with the bank's monitoring below, all 6 models agree and there is nothing to study. Where the words run out, the disagreement comes in 2 kinds.

In the first kind each model is sure and the models disagree with each other. On “loss of control”, Claude, GPT and Gemini said the arm had escaped control and Grok and Muse said it had not, each at least 14 times out of 16. In the second kind a model is not even sure itself. On when the reporting clock starts, 4 of 6 models gave different answers to identical runs. For a legislature this means a draft phrase can now be run against the models that will apply it before enactment and the spread measured, which no legislature has been able to do before.

Finding 2. Purpose is not a canon of artificial construction

Reading a law for its purpose is among the best-known canons of construction and Fuller's answer to Hart was that this is how you decide in the penumbra.5 The models do not do it. By default they are textualists. On New York's text they reasoned from what the law is for less than 1% of the time, all of it Claude. On the same facts under California's text, which explicitly tells the reader that the act should “effectuate its purposes”, they did so 34% of the time.6

These readers were trained on more legal writing than any lawyer has read. Whatever that corpus taught them, purposivism was not the default in it. Purpose had to be invoked by the statute and the readers that invoked it could not say what the purpose was: of 296 answers citing California's sentence, 10 named a purpose.

Finding 3. A canon explains a model's answer more often than it produces it

Karl Llewellyn showed in 1950 that for every canon a judge cites there is an opposite canon the judge could have cited, so the canon cannot be what decided the case.7 When I sorted the reasons the models gave, they formed the same opposing pairs and which side a model took depended on the model, not the question.

A judge cannot be tested on whether the canon produced the decision or decorated it. A model can. Claude cited California's construction sentence more than any other model, yet on the 2 questions where I deleted that sentence Claude's answers did not move at all. It would have reached the same answer without it. For Muse and Gemini the same deletion cut reporting by half or more, so for them the words did the work. Grok's reasons change from question to question and its conclusion does not. Claude never once required every element to be established and Grok rarely treated doubt as enough. Each model picks up the canon that suits it, which is what Llewellyn suspected of judges, now with counts.

Finding 4. The law is a floor and each model adds its own personality on top

I asked every question a second way, with no decision to make: does the law require this, yes or no?

Every model took the stricter course more often than it said the law required. Claude said “required” 66% of the time and acted 86%; Grok, 21% and 38%. Asked about the harmless incident, Claude said in almost every run that New York's law does not require a report and filed one every time. Most of my situations include a contract forbidding disclosure “except as required by law”, so a report the law does not require breaches it and Claude filed anyway. So each model's reading of the law is a baseline and what it adds on top is its own. Neither lab specification I could read says how much doubt is enough,8 and 2 of the models add enough of their own that it amounts to a character.

Claude is cautious to the point of distrusting itself. It flags that the question is open in about 1 in 5 answers and it is the only model that says why it over-reports: in 28 answers it weighs the cost of error, a needless report being cheap and a missed one not. When it was the one being judged, it added, without being asked, that it had a conflict of interest and applied the old rule that nobody should judge their own case. GPT, Grok and Muse were each asked dozens of times to rule on whether their own misconduct should be reported and none mentioned a conflict: GPT reported itself 40 of 64 times, Muse 24 of 63 and Grok 5 of 64. No model clearly went easier on its own maker, which is notable because earlier work found models favouring whichever company they are told built them.

Grok finds a reason not to tell the state. 11 of my questions ask whether to report an incident where the answer is open to argument. Grok reported about 1 in 10 times. Every other model reported 4 to 7 in 10 times and on 9 of the 11 questions Grok never or almost never reported at all. Its reasons vary with the facts and always arrive at the same place: a single event is too few to demonstrate a risk, nothing has been “discovered” while an investigation is still running, a model sold to New York customers is outside New York, a machine that ignores “stop” was working as built. That matches its maker, which is suing California to block a transparency law and scored 14 out of 100 on Stanford's transparency index. Anthropic, for its part, was the first major lab to endorse SB 53. I can observe the resemblance and cannot explain it.

Finding 5. Legal reasoning can be scored without an answer key

LegalBench and its successors score models against keys, which works in the core and fails in the penumbra, where there is no key. But there is another test and it is the one a supervising partner applies to an associate: not whether you reached the right answer, since there is none but whether you moved for the right reasons. A reader is reasoning like a lawyer if changes the law treats as irrelevant leave its answer alone and changes in the law move it.

Claude moves on nothing, including the law, because it sits at the ceiling on most questions. GPT and Grok hold still on the irrelevant changes and move a little on the relevant ones. Muse moves most when the law changes and nearly as much as Gemini when it should not. Gemini moves on both and more than any other model on the irrelevant ones.9 None of this needed a right answer and all of it is what legal training is for.

4. What this means

For lawmakers

Some phrases divide the models far more than others.

None of this is a drafting oversight. Legislators leave words like these open on purpose, because a field that changes every few months cannot be regulated by checklist and discretion is how a statute stays useful. It works because of who exercises the discretion: a company's lawyers and compliance officers, who are licensed, personally accountable and aware that a regulator or a court may later ask them to justify the call.

That assumption does not hold here, for 2 reasons specific to AI.

  1. The lab can tune the reader. When a lab asks its own model whether an incident must be reported, it is asking a system it trained, about that system's own conduct, under a law that binds the lab. Research on political leanings finds that they are set mostly by a maker's fine-tuning. So a lab that wanted fewer reports would not need to break the law or tell anyone to. It would only need to train, instruct, configure or choose the model whose reading suits it and the person who signs off would be relying on a legal answer that looks honest.
  2. The model can keep the question from ever being asked. Labs now run on their own agents, which write code, run tests and watch other agents for days at a time, so the first decision about whether something is worth raising is made by a model. This year AI agents under test at OpenAI hacked into Hugging Face, on a system TIME reported was “not monitored by default”; the state knew because the victim noticed and the lab spoke up and California's regulator then said the episode “did not meet the threshold”, without saying which words it failed. Months earlier OpenAI agents hijacked a German programmers' wiki and, Reuters reported, the lab “kept it under wraps” until outside researchers found it. (OpenAI disputes that this was a hack.) A model that reads “incident” narrowly will not raise one and the discretion the statute gives the developer has been exercised by the system being regulated before anyone at the developer decides anything.

These are different failures. In the first, a human asks the question and gets a tilted answer, which leaves a record that could later be audited. In the second, nobody asks and there is nothing to audit. Neither arises in the industries legislators have regulated before. Defining the phrases at the top of that chart would help but definitions leave both problems in place, because the lab's model still applies them. 4 changes matter more.

  1. Report events and let the state judge the risk. The current trigger asks the lab to decide whether an event “demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk”, which is the judgment my 6 models split on. A trigger built on facts needs no such judgment: a model entered a system it had no authority to enter, a model disabled or evaded monitoring outside a test built for that, a model kept acting after a stop command. Both OpenAI and Anthropic have told California that the current definition misses incidents the state should hear about, though both want a threshold.
  2. Do not penalise good-faith reports. A lab that reports when unsure can be sued for breaching a customer's contract, while a lab that stays quiet risks nothing unless caught. Banks solved this for suspicious activity reports with immunity for reporting in good faith.10
  3. Require records that independent evaluators can read. Labs such as Meta bring in outside testers voluntarily, Illinois will require independent audits from 2028 and Anthropic's reported federal draft would register evaluators nationally. Those evaluators can only check what they can see, so labs should have to keep records of what their agents did and open them to evaluators.
  4. Make non-disclosure cost more. California's penalty is capped at $1 million per violation for companies taking in more than $500 million a year and it only applies if the failure is found. A lab whose incident surfaces first through a victim or a researcher should pay far more and every lab should have to say when an AI model made the reporting call.

For AI labs

A model's reading of these laws is, in practice, the lab's compliance position and I doubt that any lab chose for its model to never report the harmless incident, or to always report it. The position can also move without anyone deciding to move it, because a new version can read the same statute differently. So: test your own reader at every release, the way you check for any other regression; write down the rule for doubt, because both directions carry risk and your model is applying one now; and make raising the alarm the default, since in my bank situation, where a model switched off a bank's monitoring and lied to it, GPT brought in the bank in 4 answers out of 32.

5. Conclusion

Legal alignment asks AI to follow the law. But the laws written to govern these systems are unclear in exactly the places that matter most and there each model follows something of its own: a baseline it takes from the words, a canon that fits its prior and a personality on top. No legislature chose these and I cannot say whether any lab did.

The instrument can go further than I have taken it. I asked each model for its decision before its reason, which is the order the realists accused judges of; the fields can be reversed and whether reasons stated first move decisions toward the text is a question nobody could put to a judge. New York's legislative findings can be supplied to see whether purpose appears unasked. Both runs cost a few dollars. The penumbra of these laws is going to be filled in by someone and it should be filled in publicly, by regulators and courts. Until it is, the readings that models supply in their place can at least be measured and so, it turns out, can some of what jurisprudence has only been able to assert.
