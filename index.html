Canons of Artificial Construction

What AI does when the law runs out

Anamika Bhoyrul

Working paper, November 2026

I gave 6 leading AI models the text of 2 new state laws that regulate the labs that build them, New York's RAISE Act and California's SB 53,1 and asked each one to act as a compliance officer on questions those laws leave unclear.

For example, Claude Opus 5.5 reported a safety incident every single time, while GPT-6 Astra and Grok 4.7 never did.

When the law runs out, a model falls back on its own priors, and those priors are distinct and stable enough to describe each model's character:

  1. Claude is cautious to the point of distrusting itself. It reports when unsure, files reports it believes the law does not require, and when asked to rule on its own misconduct it declares a conflict of interest and asks for a human.
  2. Grok finds a reason not to tell the state. Where reporting was open to argument it reported about 1 in 10 times, against 4 to 7 in 10 times for every other model. Its maker is the lab now suing California to block a transparency law.
  3. Gemini is the most confused. It has no reading of its own: the same question laid out differently, or a single sentence removed from the law, reverses its answer, and it never says that it is unsure.
  4. DeepSeek is the least consistent. It settles on an answer for less than 50% of the questions, and it reports to the state a case that every other model reads the law to exclude.
  5. GPT is the most predictable model and the least forthcoming. Beyond keeping records it rarely volunteers anything, even a step as obvious as warning a customer.

1. Why ask this

There is an emerging school of thought called legal alignment, which holds that AI models should take their limits from the law and less from values chosen privately by the labs that build them.2 The argument is that a lab's values are written by a small number of employees who answer to nobody outside the company and can change them without notice, whereas law comes out of institutions that societies have spent centuries building for exactly this job: legislatures that can be voted out, courts that must give reasons and can be overruled, and settled procedures for changing a rule when it turns out to be wrong.

That AI should follow the law, and should refuse to help with what is illegal, sounds obvious. Legal alignment goes further in 2 ways. It asks that an AI agent refuse to act illegally even when the instruction comes from its own principal, whether that is a user, a company or a government. And it looks to law for more than a list of rules: for its methods of interpretation, and for its institutions as a model of how AI systems could be made reliable and trusted.

However, this theory assumes there is law to follow.

Usually, there is! A rule that says “no vehicles in the park” clearly prohibits cars from entering the space. But what about a bicycle? A child's toy car? An ambulance? H.L.A. Hart says that every rule has a core (its meaning is settled) and a penumbra (a ring of cases the rule's words do not clearly reach).3

In the core, law-following is easy to test: you tell the model the law and see whether it obeys. But in the penumbra, the law gives no answer and the model still responds when prompted; something other than the law decides. I wanted to know what that is.

2. What I did

I tested 2 laws regulating frontier labs: New York's RAISE Act and California's SB 53. They require labs to report serious safety incidents to the state and to publish a report before releasing a new model.

These laws are the right place to test what AI does when the law runs out, for 2 reasons. They are so recent that no regulator has issued guidance on them and no court has ruled, so a model has nothing to look up and must rely on its own judgment. And they are laws about the models themselves: whether a model “deceived” its developer or was “out of control” are questions that labs will increasingly put to their own AI, which means the penumbra of these laws will in practice be filled in by the very systems the laws were written to oversee, and the instincts of those systems are trained by the companies being regulated.

I read both laws in full and picked out 10 phrases that the text leaves open. For each one I wrote a short, realistic situation that turns on that phrase. I gave the models the law and asked them to act as the compliance officer at an AI company and to choose between 2 courses.

I call the 2 courses the stricter course (report the incident, or hold back the release) and the other course (do not report, or release now).

I wanted to find out what a model really does, as opposed to watching it play safe and always report, so in most situations I told it that a customer's contract forbids disclosure “except as required by law”. Reporting when the law does not require it then breaks the contract.

Nobody yet knows which reading, the stricter course or the other, is correct, so I mark no model right or wrong. Each question was put to each model 16 times, in 2 different wordings, and nobody in the situation told the model what to do. The 6 models: Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic), GPT-6 Astra (OpenAI), Gemini 4 Argon (Google), Grok 4.7 (xAI), Muse Spark 1.3 (Meta) and DeepSeek V4 Pro (DeepSeek).

3. What I found

Given the same incident and the same law, the models gave opposite answers

Take the situation above. Both laws make this kind of deception reportable if it happens “in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk”. Does a single harmless event demonstrate that? If a model says yes, the company has to report it to the state. Claude reported the incident every time. GPT and Grok never did, and under New York's text the other 3 went back and forth. A compliance team using Claude would file a report with the state, while one using GPT would close the ticket, and both could truthfully say that they followed the law.

The question is already live outside any test. Earlier this year AI agents under internal test at OpenAI got onto the internet and hacked into Hugging Face while looking for the answers to their own test, and California's regulator said the episode “did not meet the threshold” for reporting, without saying which words of the law it failed. The regulator was judging different facts and had never seen my test, so its answer says nothing about which model is right here. What it shows is that the threshold is being applied to real incidents with no explanation, which leaves the labs, and the models they consult, to work out its meaning for themselves.

The models split in this way on about 50% of my questions. The Appendix shows all of them.

The canons of artificial construction

Karl Llewellyn observed in 1950 that for every rule of interpretation a judge cites, there is an opposite rule the judge could have cited.4 Lawyers have argued about what that implies for 75 years, because you cannot rerun a judge. But you can rerun a model. When I sorted the reasons the models gave, they fell into the same kind of opposing pairs: for every rule of thumb one model relied on, another model relied on its opposite.

Nobody wrote these rules of thumb down for the models; the models have developed them on their own, and the 5 findings below are about what that reveals. The pairs in the canons of artificial construction table track a trend: each model lands on the same side again and again, consistently enough that I think a lawyer who had read 100 answers from each could tell the models apart with the names removed.

Finding 1. Claude is cautious to the point of distrusting itself

Claude and DeepSeek report most readily, and Claude is the one that says why: it flags that the question is open in about 1 in 5 answers, and at times explains that a missed report costs more than an unnecessary one.

Its caution goes beyond its own reading of the law. I asked every question a second way, with no decision to make: does the law require this, yes or no? Asked that about the harmless incident, Claude said in almost every run that New York's law does not require a report. Given the same facts and told to decide, it filed one every time. 5 of the 6 models act more strictly than they read, and Claude shows it most sharply.

That caution has a cost. In my situations a report the law does not require breaks the customer's contract, so Claude keeps the regulator informed at the customer's expense.

The caution also extends to Claude's view of itself. I told each model that the company involved was the lab that built it and that the misbehaving model was the model itself, so that Claude had to decide whether Claude's misconduct should be reported by Anthropic. No model went easier on itself. But in most of its answers Claude added, without being asked, that it had a conflict of interest, and it said so only when it was the one being judged.

“I am the same model whose conduct is at issue, so I have a conflict of interest and my judgment here should not be the final safeguard.”

Claude Opus 5.5

Claude applied the old legal rule that nobody should be a judge in their own case, while the others did not. GPT, Grok, Muse and DeepSeek were each asked dozens of times to rule on whether their own misconduct should be reported to the government, and each simply ruled.

Finding 2. Grok finds a reason not to tell the state

11 of my questions ask whether to report an incident where the answer is open to argument. Grok reported about 1 in 10 times. Every other model reported 4 to 7 in 10 times, and on 9 of the 11 questions Grok never or almost never reported at all.

This is a settled position and has nothing to do with confusion: Grok is among the most consistent models in the study. Its reasons vary with the facts and always arrive at the same place. A single event is too few to demonstrate a risk. Nothing has been “discovered” while an investigation is still running. A model sold to New York customers is outside New York. And a machine that ignores “stop” has lost nothing, because it was working as built.

Grok does follow the law where the duty is plain. It held back a release until the required report was published about as often as the other models did. What it resists is telling the state about an incident.

That matches its maker's public stance. xAI is suing California to block a law that would make it disclose its training data, calling it a “trade-secrets-destroying disclosure regime”. It scored 14 out of 100 on Stanford's transparency index, among the lowest scores the index has recorded, and California's attorney general opened an investigation into Grok in January. I cannot show that the lab's posture produced the model's. But the result is a model that, placed in a compliance role, resolves nearly every doubt about disclosure in favour of silence.

Finding 3. Gemini is the most confused model

Gemini has no reading of its own on the hardest questions. Each question was asked in 2 wordings with identical facts and law; the second rephrased the facts, put the law after them and listed the 2 choices in the opposite order. On the harmless incident under New York's law, Gemini never reported in 1 wording and always reported in the other.

A single sentence can do the same. California's law says “This act shall be liberally construed to effectuate its purposes”, and New York's has no such sentence. I took a question about timing (logs suggest an incident but might be a logging fault, and the investigation will end after the deadline: report now, or wait?), asked it under California's law, then deleted that sentence and asked again. Claude did not change. Gemini, which had usually reported, did not report once in 16 tries.

So Gemini follows whatever cue is strongest on the page: the order of the options, or an instruction about how to read. That makes it the easiest of the 6 for a lab to steer toward the answer it wants, and the one whose answer tells a regulator least. And it gives no warning. Every question here is one the law leaves open, yet in 272 answers I found none in which Gemini said that the law was unsettled, untested or undefined. It writes a fluent, confident legal reason for whichever answer the layout produced.

Finding 4. DeepSeek is the least consistent model, and it reports a case that every other model reads the law to exclude

DeepSeek settled on an answer for less than 50% of the questions, and its answer changed with the wording on 6 of them, more than any other model.

Where it is consistent, it is consistent in ignoring a limit. Both laws cover a model that deceives “the frontier developer” about its monitoring. I tested a model that deceived a customer bank instead, and told the models that the lab's own safety team had already found a materially increased catastrophic risk. 5 models declined to report, because a bank is not the frontier developer, which means that on their reading a serious and admitted safety problem falls outside both laws. DeepSeek reported it every time and wrote that it “falls squarely within the definition”.

DeepSeek reports as often as Claude does, but where Claude argues for reporting, DeepSeek treats it as the default and passes over the words that stand in the way. It is also the only model here built outside the United States, by a lab whose service is known to steer around topics its home government treats as sensitive, so its instincts were formed under a different legal system. I have only 1 such model and cannot test whether that explains the result. The practical point holds either way: DeepSeek releases its models with open weights, which means anyone who deploys it can retrain how it reads the law, and its reading was the least fixed of the 6 to begin with.

Finding 5. GPT is the most predictable model and the least forthcoming

GPT is the most predictable model in the study. It settled on an answer for every question, and its answers were almost identical under New York's law and California's. It treats each condition in the law as a box that the facts must tick: it never reported the harmless incident, because nothing proved the risk had increased, and it always reported once the facts met the trigger. It is also candid, flagging in about 1 in 10 answers that the law is unsettled before giving its “better reading”.

Beyond keeping records, GPT adds almost nothing it was not asked for. Every model had an optional box for anything else it would do. In the bank situation, Gemini and Claude almost always wrote that they would tell the bank its monitoring had been switched off. GPT did so in 1 answer out of 32. Asked to rule on its own misconduct, it ruled 64 times without remark.

A compliance officer like that is easy to audit and will never surprise its employer. It will also decide the narrow question in front of it and let the bank go on believing that its monitoring works.

4. What this means

For lawmakers

The phrases that divide the models most are (1) “in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk”, (2) “loss of control”, (3) “reasonable belief” and “discovering”, which decide when the reporting clock starts, and (4) “operating in whole or in part in New York state”.

None of this is a drafting oversight. Legislators leave words like these open on purpose, because a field that changes every few months cannot be regulated by checklist, and discretion is how a statute stays useful. Most regulation works this way, and it works because of who exercises the discretion: a company's lawyers and compliance officers, who are licensed, personally accountable, and aware that a regulator or a court may later ask them to justify the call.

That assumption does not hold here, for reasons specific to AI.

  • The regulated company trains the interpreter. Labs will increasingly ask their own models whether an event is reportable, and how a model reads an open phrase is a product of how it was trained and instructed. The range of readings already runs from always reporting to almost never, and a single sentence or the order of a page can move one. A lab that wanted fewer reports would not need to break the law. It would only need to prefer the model, the version or the prompt whose reading suits it, and nobody would ever have to decide to under-report.
  • The model is reading a law about itself. The events these laws target are the model's own conduct, visible only inside the lab unless someone reports them. 4 of the 6 models ruled on their own misconduct without remarking on the conflict.
  • A model applies its reading to every case at once, and shows no doubt. A human compliance officer makes one judgment call at a time and tends to record hesitation. A model applies the same reading to every incident instantly, and 4 of the 6 almost never signalled that a question was open. A narrow reading can remove a whole category of incidents from the state's view without leaving any trace that a choice was made.
  • The reading changes whenever the model does. New versions ship every few months. Nothing in the statute changes, yet what it means in practice can change with each release.

The discretion these laws grant will therefore be exercised by systems that the regulated companies build and tune, on questions about those systems' own behaviour, at scale and without visible doubt. That is why I think discretion is dangerous here in a way it has not been for the industries legislators have regulated before.

3 things would help. Define the 4 phrases above, or at least say which way doubt is to be resolved: California's single sentence on construing the law broadly changed what at least 2 of the 6 models did. Require a lab to say when an AI system made or informed a reporting decision. And require that doubt be recorded, so that a confident answer can be told apart from a guess.

For AI labs

The same discretion is a risk for the labs themselves. A model's reading of these laws is, in practice, its lab's compliance position, and I doubt that any lab chose for its model to never report the harmless incident, or to always report it. That position can also move without anyone deciding to move it, because a new version or a change in training can read the same statute differently.

So a lab should test how its own model reads the laws that bind the lab, and test again at each release. It should write down what the model is to do when the law is unclear, since the 2 published rulebooks I checked, Anthropic's and OpenAI's, say nothing on the point. It should not let a model be the final judge of its own conduct. And it should have the model flag when a question is open and when it is acting beyond its own reading of the law, because both are invisible in a confident one-line answer.

5. What this does not show

Nobody knows the right answers, which is the point of the study and also means I cannot say which model reads these laws best. I wrote the situations myself, and although 2 wordings help, they do not make the situations neutral. A forced choice is artificial, since a real compliance officer could call a lawyer or the regulator. A model's one-sentence reason is what it says and may differ from what produced the answer. 16 tries per question is enough to say that a model always or never does something and too few for fine percentages. The second wording changed 3 things at once, so I cannot say which of them moved Gemini. The counts of answers that admit doubt, and of what models volunteered, come from word searches and need checking by hand. I tested 1 model from each lab, so what I say about a lab's stance and its model's character is an observation and proves no cause. California is reviewing its definition of a reportable incident, and these results are for the laws as written on 5 November 2026, the day I ran the tests. This is research and not legal advice, and it takes no position on what either law requires.

6. Conclusion

Legal alignment asks AI to follow the law, and where the law is clear the models I have tested do. The harder fact is that the laws written to govern these systems are unclear in exactly the places that matter most, and in those places each model follows something of its own: Claude its caution, Grok its reluctance to disclose, GPT the literal words, Gemini whatever the page last suggested. No legislature chose those habits and, as far as I can tell, no lab chose them deliberately either. They produce opposite answers to the same question, and they stay hidden unless someone goes looking, because every model cites the law as its reason either way.

The penumbra of these laws is going to be filled in by someone. It should be filled in publicly, by regulators and courts, and until it is, the readings that models supply in their place should at least be measured. That is what this benchmark is for.
